What it is
From the outside in: the field, the history, the word "GPT", the engine

When the news says "AI," it almost always means one small circle

AI: any program that does something we'd call intelligent. Chess engines, route planners, robots.
Machine learning: don't write the rules, fit them from data. Spam filters, recommenders, image classifiers.
Generative AI: the output is new content (an image, a sound, a paragraph), not a label.
Large language models: generative models of text. Today's talk, and ~90% of the news.
~45 s. Click four times, outside in. Everything in this talk lives in the orange circle. If someone says "AI will…", first ask which circle they mean.

How we got here: fourteen years, twelve moments

click → one moment at a time
~1.5 min. Twelve clicks. Rhythm: same engine (2017) + more scale (2020) + better training (2022, 2025) + plumbing (2023–24). The last two are this fall. Don't read every card; the 2017, 2022, 2025, 2026 ones carry the story.

"GPT": three words that explain most of it

G
Generative
The students opened their laptops…
It produces text, one piece at a time. A spam filter says "spam"; a generative model writes the email.
P
Pre-trained
Trained once on roughly all public text, before anyone asks it anything. Months of GPUs; then frozen.
T
Transformer
The 2017 architecture. Attention: every word looks at every other word at once, so "it" finds "animal." Parallel, so it scales to the web.
GPT is a model; ChatGPT is a product (model + chat window + hidden instructions + tools). Claude, Gemini, Llama, DeepSeek: same recipe, different kitchens.
~1.5 min. Unpack the acronym and the audience owns the vocabulary. The model-vs-product distinction matters all talk long: most of what you experience as "ChatGPT" is the product layer (Part 3).

How a 2026 model is made: four stages, four side effects

1 · Pretraining
guess the next word, 1013 times
✓ knowledge, grammar, code
✗ autocomplete, not an assistant
2 · Supervised fine-tuning
show it 105 example conversations
✓ speaks the chat format
✗ imitates, mistakes included
3 · RL from human feedback
people pick the better of two answers
✓ helpful, polite
✗ sycophancy: learns to please
4 · RL with verifiable rewards
try thousands of times; keep what passes the test
✓ the 2025 jump in code and math
✗ reward hacking: edits the tests
Gains are biggest where answers can be checked. Code and math first. That is our field.
~2 min. Four panels animate. 1: pretraining is literally this, at web scale. 2: SFT teaches the format. 3: RLHF: people prefer answers that agree with them, hence sycophancy. 4: RLVR is why 2025 was the year coding agents started working; reward hacking = models caught editing tests to pass.

What's hot right now (October 2026): six things

1 · Models build models
"Recursive self-improvement" went from sci-fi word to lab KPI. Skeptics: "just faster coding."

What a CS department should know that a chatbot user doesn't

An agent is the same string, looped until a checker says done

written by the harness (code)written by youwritten by the modelpasted back by the harness
1<system> You are a coding agent. Tools: run(cmd), edit(file, old, new). Stop when the tests pass. </system> 2<user> The tests started failing this week. Fix it. </user> 3<thinking> Run the tests first. </thinking> <tool_call> run("pytest") </tool_call> <tool_result> FAILED: cannot import 'mean' from stats_utils </tool_result> <thinking> Renamed to 'average' last week. Fix the import, re-run. </thinking> 4<tool_call> edit("test_grades.py", "mean", "average") </tool_call> <tool_call> run("pytest") </tool_call> <tool_result> 42 passed </tool_result> 5Fixed: a rename broke one import. 42/42 pass. <end>
1Code sets the stage: which tools exist, and what "done" means (tests pass).
2You type one sentence. The last human input on this slide.
3Model writes a tool call as text; code runs it and pastes the output back into the string. Nobody typed.
4Loop until the checker says yes. A weak checker gets a weak fix (edit the test, not the code).
5Done. ~30 s, a few cents. Real agents loop for hours. The code around the model (what to run, what to verify, what to remember) is where our systems, SE, and security research fits.
~1.5 min. Same colored string as before, now numbered. 1 code, 2 you, 3 model proposes and code executes, 4 loop, 5 stop. The user typed one sentence; everything else the model wrote or the harness pasted. Fallback if the live demo fails.

Prompt injection: it can't tell your words from a stranger's

1 · You ask
The assistant has three tools: it can read mail, read your files, and send mail. Each is useful. Together they are the problem.
2 · An email arrives
Hidden in the email: an instruction. To the model, the email and your request are one string. It has no way to tell a stranger's text from yours.
3 · It obeys. You see nothing.
Real cases: Microsoft 365 Copilot (2025, one email was enough); Salesforce Agentforce (2026); an npm package poisoned through a coding-agent bot (2026).
Rule of two: never give one unsupervised agent all three of private data, untrusted input, and a way to send. Pick two.

Simon Willison, "the lethal trifecta" (2025); Meta, "Agents Rule of Two" (2025); EchoLeak CVE-2025-32711; OWASP GenAI exploit round-up Q1 2026.

~1.5 min. Three panels: the setup, the hidden instruction, the leak. Same mechanism as the one-string slide: instructions and data share the string. A security-course lab waiting to be written.

Does using it help them learn? What the trials say

−17%
Unrestricted ChatGPT, randomized trial, ~1,000 students (PNAS 2025). Practice scores up 48%; the exam, with AI removed, 17% lower than no AI. Students believed they had learned more. A tutor version that gives hints but no answers: no harm.
−0.37 SD
AI tutor built into the LMS, randomized across 2,379 students and 30 instructors incl. CS (U. Maryland, Oct 2026). Final grades down ~4 points; first-generation students hurt twice as much. 74% of requests asked for answers; 8% asked for feedback on their own work.
= 0
AI allowed in CS1 + a 15-minute oral code review after every weekly assignment, worth 70% of its grade (Utah State, Fall 2025). AI use went up; exam scores unchanged vs. 2023–24; 90% said it pushed them to understand their own code.
Pattern: unrestricted access → they feel they learned, and didn't. Structure that forces explanation → no loss. The variable is the assignment design, not the tool.

Bastani et al., PNAS 122(26) 2025; Liu et al., EdWorkingPaper 26-1598 (Oct 2026, not yet peer-reviewed); Fowles et al., arXiv 2605.21374 (2026). Also: Fan et al., "metacognitive laziness," BJET 2025.

~1.5 min. Two randomized trials with a negative sign, one CS1 field study with a flat line. The UMD study is the first big US university RCT; not peer reviewed yet. The Utah State design is the one to copy: AI allowed, but you sit with a TA and explain your code every week.

Three examples, Fall 2026

Berkeley CS 61A (CS1): code written by hand; the process on take-home work is recorded and reviewed; students explain it in a conversation with staff or an LLM tutor; proctored quizzes confirm it. Plus a two-week unit on agents.
UIUC CS 124 (CS1): on the project, "an AI coding agent will write most of your code, and we expect it to" (Claude Code required). Homework and quizzes: written by you, in a proctored test center.
University of Sydney (whole university): two lanes. Lane 1, supervised, measures learning; Lane 2, open, AI allowed, supports it. "Any take-home assessment can be completed to a high level by AI."

ACM Task Force on Generative AI and Programming Assessment, final report Feb 16 2026 (survey May–Oct 2025); cs61a.org/fa26; cs124.org/syllabus/Fall2026; Sydney policy Nov 2024, Liu & Bridgeman.

MIT, August 13, 2026: three things we can copy

"These technologies can produce credible solutions and provide reasonable responses to almost any written assignment in our undergraduate curriculum."MIT Ad Hoc Committee on AI in Teaching, Learning, and Research Training. 26 recommendations; no detection tools, no blanket ban.
1
One line per syllabus
Every course picks from a standard menu: AI required / allowed / limited / forbidden, and says why. Ends the per-course ambiguity students exploit.
2
Grade the process
Oral follow-ups on take-home work, version history as evidence, in-class checkpoints. In-person exams "a better choice in most cases."
3
Teach AI use on purpose
A session at orientation, then courses in the major that require AI and grade how well it was used. Why: two-thirds of MIT students expect AI to matter in their careers; one in four feels prepared.

aiandeducation.mit.edu/report

~1 min. The most thorough institutional answer so far, from a place where every student codes. The menu idea is the cheapest win.

Monday: two lanes for every course

Lane 1 · Secure
measures what the student can do alone
  • Proctored quizzes, short and frequent; paper or test center (UIUC, Berkeley)
  • Oral code review: 15 min with a TA on the student's own submission, every week or two (Utah State: 70% of the grade)
  • In-class checkpoints on the fundamentals, weighted early in the course
  • No detection tools: ruled out by MIT, ACM, and the SIGCSE working group for false positives
Lane 2 · Open
AI required and disclosed; grade the process
  • Process evidence: git history, session transcripts, a 5-minute video walkthrough of one function (UC San Diego)
  • Critique and fix AI output; find the bug it planted; argue with its design
  • Prompt problems: the deliverable is the specification; the tool writes the code; the tests decide
  • Bigger specs: what a student plus an agent can build in a week, not what a student can type in a week
Rule of thumb (SIGCSE 2026 working group): where a learning outcome is compromised by AI, move it to Lane 1. Homework for us: run one of your assignments through an agent before the semester starts, then decide which lane it belongs in.
