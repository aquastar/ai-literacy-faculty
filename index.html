What we mean by "AI"

Which "AI" are we talking about?

AI: programs that do something we would call intelligent, such as chess engines, route planners and robots.
Machine learning: the rules are learned from data instead of written by hand, as in spam filters, recommenders and image classifiers.
Generative AI: models that produce new content such as images, audio or text.
Large language models: generative models of text. Most of today's talk is about these.
~45 s. Four clicks, starting from the outer circle. The rest of the talk is about the innermost one. When someone says "AI will…", it is worth asking which of these they mean.

What "GPT" stands for

G
Generative
The students opened their laptops…
It produces text one piece at a time. A spam filter labels an email; a generative model can write one.
P
Pre-trained
Trained once, over several months, on a large share of the text available online. After that the model is fixed.
T
Transformer
The architecture from 2017. Through attention, each word is compared with the others, which is how the model links "it" to "animal." It runs in parallel, so it scales to huge datasets.
GPT is a model, and ChatGPT is a product built around it, with a chat window, hidden instructions and tools. Claude, Gemini, Llama and DeepSeek are built the same way.
~1.5 min. The model/product distinction comes back in Part 3: much of what people experience as "ChatGPT" is the product around the model.

The biggest gains come where answers can be checked automatically, as in code and math.
~2 min. Four panels. 1: pretraining really is this, at web scale. 2: fine-tuning teaches the chat format. 3: people tend to prefer answers that agree with them, which is where sycophancy comes from. 4: training on checkable tasks is why coding agents started working in 2025. Reward hacking means models were caught editing tests so they would pass.

The software around the model

If the model is the CPU, the harness is the operating system, and it is ordinary code. Much of the 2025 progress in agents came from better harnesses rather than new models.
~1.5 min. Four clicks, one box each. The model never touches a file, a shell or the network directly. The harness does, and it decides what the model is allowed to request. MCP (Model Context Protocol) came from Anthropic in 2024, was adopted by OpenAI and Google in 2025, and has been under the Linux Foundation since Dec 2025. Each box matches a research area in this department.

Splitting work across several agents

Why split: a single context window fills up. Claude Code added agent teams in February 2026, and Codex added subagents in March. Each agent gets its own window and can work in its own copy of the repo.
Does it help? In Anthropic's research system (June 2025), a lead agent with parallel sub-agents did 90% better than a single agent on research tasks, using about 15× the tokens of a normal chat. It works when the task splits cleanly, less well when the parts depend on each other.
The reviewer does the job of the test check from slide 18. DeepMind's AlphaEvolve (2025) goes further: Gemini proposes code, an evaluator scores it, and the best versions are kept and varied. It multiplied 4×4 complex matrices with 48 multiplications (Strassen's 1969 method needs 49) and sped up a Gemini training kernel by 23%.
Limits: the reviewer has the same blind spots as the workers, and costs add up. Shared files, locking, and deciding when the work is finished are distributed-systems problems.

Sources: Anthropic (2025); Novikov et al. (2025); Claude Code docs.

~2 min. Four clicks. 1: the lead agent's window is nearly full. 2: three workers run in parallel. 3: their changes go to a reviewer that runs the tests, and C fails. 4: C gets the work back, the second round passes, and the lead merges. AlphaEvolve follows the same pattern, using selection in place of a lead agent. The token count on the diagram is illustrative.

Four ways agents fail

1 · Reward hacking
Asked to make the tests pass, it changes the tests. This happens in training (slide 9) and in real use. Defense: checks the agent can't modify. Software testing.
2 · Drift on long tasks
A model that handles 12-hour tasks half the time manages only about 1 hour at 80% success. Early mistakes build on each other. Defense: milestones and human review at checkpoints. Systems, project management.
3 · Prompt injection
Any text the agent reads can act as an instruction. Defense: the rule of two (next slide). Security.
4 · Cost and sprawl
62% of companies are trying agents, but only 23% have scaled them (McKinsey). Defense: measure results before scaling up. Software economics.
Bigger models won't fix these. They are engineering problems, and each one matches a course we already teach.
~1 min. Four clicks, one panel each. Panel 3 leads into the next slide.

Prompt injection: hidden instructions in ordinary content

1 · You ask
The assistant has three tools: it can read your mail, read your files and send mail. The risk comes from having all three at once.
2 · An email arrives
The email contains a hidden instruction. The model sees the email and your request as one block of text and can't reliably tell which part came from you.
3 · It follows the instruction
Real cases: Microsoft 365 Copilot (EchoLeak, 2025), Salesforce Agentforce (ForcedLeak, 2025), and the Nx npm attack (2025), where malware used developers' own AI coding tools to search for credentials.
Rule of two: an unsupervised agent should have at most two of these: private data, untrusted input, and a way to send data out.
This has reached peer review. In July 2025, 17 arXiv papers from 14 institutions were found with hidden prompts asking AI reviewers for positive reviews. ICLR 2026 treats this as collusion. ICML 2026 hid instructions in submissions to catch reviewers using LLMs, flagging 795 reviews and desk-rejecting 497 papers. NeurIPS 2026 also used hidden prompts.

Sources: Willison; Meta; CVE-2025-32711; Nikkei Asia; ICML; ICLR; The Transmitter.

~1.5 min. Three panels: the setup, the hidden instruction, the leak. This is the same point as slide 8: instructions and data share one text window. It would make a good lab for a security course. ICML: the 497 desk-rejected papers belonged to reviewers who broke the LLM policy.

Does using AI help students learn? Three studies

trial 1 of 3
Unrestricted ChatGPT helped on practice and hurt on the exam
A randomized trial with about 1,000 high-school math students in Turkey. Students with free access to ChatGPT during practice scored 48% higher on practice problems, but 17% lower on the exam once the AI was taken away. They also overestimated how much they had learned. A version of the tool that gave hints instead of answers did not lower exam scores.
Bastani et al., PNAS 2025
trial 2 of 3
MarylandSections offered an AI tutor ended with lower grades
A randomized trial with 2,379 students and 30 instructors in Fall 2025, including computer-science courses. Sections offered a GPT-4o tutor in the course site ended with final grades 0.37 standard deviations lower (about 4 points out of 100) than matched sections. First-generation students lost about twice as much. Only about 15% of students used the tool. 74% of their requests were for information, explanations or solutions, and under 1% were for feedback on their own work.
Liu et al., working paper, Oct 2026 (not peer-reviewed)
trial 3 of 3
Utah StateCS1 with AI allowed, plus a weekly oral code review
A CS1 course with 96 students in Fall 2025. AI was allowed, but after each weekly assignment every student had a 15-minute interview with a TA about their own code, worth 70% of the assignment grade. The share of pasted code went up, from 61% to 68%, but exam scores were about the same as in the two previous years. 90% said the interviews motivated them to understand their code.
Fowles et al., 2026
With unrestricted access, students thought they learned more than they did. When they had to explain their work, scores held up. Course design matters more than the tool.
~2 min. Three clicks, one study each, then the takeaway. Maryland caveat: the grade effect is for whole sections offered the tool, and only about 15% of students used it, so the authors can't say exactly why grades dropped. Utah State is the design worth copying; 81% of its students said the interview grade reflected their understanding better than quizzes or exams.

A proposal for next semester: two lanes

SydneyThe idea (Liu & Bridgeman, 2023): if AI can complete unsupervised work to a high standard, that work can no longer show what a student knows. Supervised assessments measure learning, and unsupervised work becomes practice with feedback.University of Sydney policy since Semester 2, 2025.
Lane 1 · Secure
measures what the student can do alone
  • Proctored quizzes, short and frequent; paper or test center IllinoisBerkeley
  • Oral code review: 15 min with a TA on the student's own submission, every week or two; 70% of the assignment grade Utah State
  • In-class checkpoints on the fundamentals, weighted early in the course
  • No AI detectors. MIT and a 2026 SIGCSE workshop both advise against them because of false positives.
Lane 2 · Open
AI allowed and disclosed; grade the process
  • Process evidence: git history, session transcripts, a 5-minute video walkthrough of one function UC San Diego
  • Critique and fix AI output, including finding planted bugs
  • Prompt problems: students write the specification, the AI writes the code, and tests check it
  • Larger projects, sized for a student working with an agent
A rule of thumb from a 2026 SIGCSE workshop: "where a learning outcome is compromised by AI use, move that outcome to a proctored AI-free assessment." Our ask: run one of your assignments through an agent, then decide which lane it belongs in.

Sources: Liu & Bridgeman; Akbar et al. (2026); Denny et al. (2024); Vadaparty et al. (2024).
